Drop in a photo. Hear what it becomes.

The app takes the photo apart in front of you and plays it while it does. What comes out is an instrument that follows that picture: the same photo always makes the same sound, and a different photo makes a different one.

02How it works

Not noise. An instrument, built from the picture.

Each part of the picture sets one part of a tuned instrument. Its rows become waves, its pattern becomes the tones of an object you strike, its columns become the tone that sings. Every key plays in tune; different photos sound different, and similar photos sound related.

RedSynth Pad doesn't find a hidden sound in your photo. It's a mapping designed for the app: the picture's patterns, columns and colour each set one part of an instrument. Where it uses physics, it says so: the struck tones are modal synthesis, and a space is a model block whose vibrations are solved, not guessed.

03The pad

Play it with your hands.

No keyboard needed. On iPad the screen is the instrument, ten fingers wide; on iPhone it's the same pad in your hand. On a Mac it's the trackpad you already have.

Or bring a keyboard. Play the whole synth from a MIDI keyboard with no trackpad at all. Or play the keys with one hand and the trackpad as the Touch FX pad with the other, at the same time. Loops and beats are built in: a drum kit, a sixteen-pad sampler and a loop deck that launches on the bar.

04Space

Two photos make a space.

Drop two photos in together, or add a second to the first, and they become the front and the side of a block. A mallet strikes it and it rings in its own notes. A loop of light flies round inside it, and what the loop passes through is the tone you hold.

Move the loop, and the sound moves

  • Drag empty space to turn the room. Pinch to come closer.
  • Drag the loop to move it; drag its bead to make it bigger and brighter.
  • Tap the cloud to strike it right there.
  • Press harder, with a Pencil, a flat fingertip or a deep press on the trackpad, and the loop sinks into the second photo.
  • A third photo is the view from above. Here it's the floor: the bridge as a tiny planet.
  • In Play view the space is the whole stage, turned and played by hand.

Three pictures made with maths, drawn by the app's renderer as Play view shows a space. The cloud takes about 3.5 thousandths of a second a frame on an M4 Mac, which leaves room for 120 frames a second. iPad timings haven't been measured yet.

05Sculpt

Then change it with a brush.

Sculpt opens what the sound is made of. On one photo you paint the picture. On a space you paint the block itself, in materials that are the engine's own weight and stiffness. You hear every stroke while you make it.

On a space: paint materials

  • Lead is heavy and soft: tones fall. Crystal is light and stiff: tones rise. Iron, Wood and Air sit between.
  • The brush goes down to one point of the block. A glass opens over a fine brush so you can aim it.
  • The eyedropper picks up whatever is under your finger.
  • Touch a tone's line and the block shows how that tone moves it: warm one way, cool the other, dark where it stays still.
  • When the brush lifts, the block's tones are solved exactly for the engine's model of the block, in about 1.2 seconds on an M4 Mac.

Drawn by RedSynth Pad's own renderer and views. A photo you never paint and a space you never sculpt sound exactly as before.

Find your way in 3D, and make one point count

  • Every way of moving is a button on the space: Paint, Turn, Move; Front, Side, Top, Reset; nearer and further; a depth slider.
  • A 3D cursor shows exactly where the brush will land, with lines to the walls.
  • Weight makes one point a small heavy bead, like a blob of putty on a bar. Pin holds the block at one point, like a nail holding it there.
  • After each stroke, one line says which tones moved and by how many cents, or that nothing an ear can tell moved.
  • Before and After strike the block as it was and as it is. Watch it ring shows it moving, slowed down.

06The synth

A whole synthesizer underneath.

You never have to touch it. But it's there, written from scratch. Edit opens every knob of it in the app, and every part shows up as a control your music app can see and automate.

Four big knobs do the work

Tone is brighter. Space is bigger. Grit is dirtier. Wobble is moving. Each one turns several things inside the synth at once, so a sound changes the way you'd ask for it out loud.

  • 144 sounds in eight groups, each with a line that says what it is, and a search. Your photo sounds sit above them.
  • Two oscillators and a sub, two filters, drive, unison, FM, ring mod, a wavefolder, noise, two LFOs.
  • Six effects in one chain: crush, distortion, chorus, phaser, delay, reverb.
  • A drum kit of nine synthesized drums, and a sixteen-slot sampler.
  • Edit, every knob: six pages (Sound, Motion, Effects, Photo, Drums, Setup), a search, and a line of help under each control. Its own window on a Mac, a panel beside the pad on an iPad.
  • Help on every control: hold a finger on a knob and it tells you what it does.

07In your music apps

The same instrument, as an Audio Unit.

RedSynth Pad includes an AUv3 extension, the same engine as the app, not a smaller one. It is a standard Audio Unit instrument for GarageBand and other apps that host Audio Unit instruments, on iPhone, iPad and Mac. So far it has been checked in GarageBand on the Mac.

  • The pad is inside the plugin, so you play the photo by touch right in the plugin's window.
  • The four macros, the scan line and the effect pad are parameters your host can record and automate.
  • Your photo is saved with the project. Open the song next month and the instrument is there.
  • It follows the host's tempo: delay, LFOs and loops keep the beat.

10Questions

Before you ask.

Do I need to know anything about synthesizers?

No. Choose a photo, play the pad, turn Tone, Space, Grit and Wobble. Everything else is optional, and every control explains itself when you hold a finger on it.

Can I get at every knob?

Yes. Edit opens every control of the sound: a window of its own on a Mac, a panel beside the pad on an iPad, a sheet under the pad on an iPhone. In GarageBand or another host the same controls are in the plugin's parameter list. See Edit.

Does it use AI?

No. There is no AI in RedSynth Pad: no machine-learning models, no cloud, nothing generated. Every sound is made by fixed maths and signal processing from your photo, so the same photo, touches and knobs always make the same instrument. You play it; it doesn't play for you.

Is it a filter that turns a photo into noise?

No. The photo's pattern, columns, colour and texture each set a specific part of a tuned instrument, so every key plays in tune; different photos sound different, and similar photos sound related. How it works shows each step with the engine's own numbers, and the guide's photo chapter says what each part does as you play.

Does it work on iPhone?

Yes. It's the same instrument, laid out for the phone: upright, the photo on top and the pad below it with its modes as tabs along the bottom; on its side, the pad at full height. One purchase covers iPhone, iPad and Mac.

Which Macs?

Macs with Apple silicon running macOS 15 or later. Any trackpad works as the pad; a Force Touch trackpad adds pressure. A mouse plays one finger.

Which music apps can load it?

It's a standard AUv3 instrument for GarageBand and other apps that host Audio Unit instruments, on iPhone, iPad and Mac. So far it has been checked in GarageBand on the Mac. It passes Apple's own Audio Unit validation.

Is Sculpt in the app?

Yes: the brush on one photo and on a space, the materials, the eyedropper, the glass over a fine brush and the tones as shapes. The next round (on-screen 3D controls, Weight and Pin, the Tones page, the fingerprint, sharing a sound and exporting a sample pack) is built in the Mac development window and is not in RedSynth Pad yet. It will arrive as a free update, and this page marks it "coming" until it does.

Is every sound unique?

The honest version: the same photos, touches and knobs always make the same sound. Change them and the sound changes. Small changes make small differences, sometimes too small to hear. The page gives two counts, with the maths shown: a lower bound on the sculpts you could save, and a rough count of settings far enough apart to hear. They are counts, not listening tests, and nothing beyond them is claimed.

No.
