Press kit

RedSynth Pad is not released yet. Everything here may be used in coverage. For a review copy before launch, write to Seth.

In one sentence

RedSynth Pad turns a photo into a synthesizer you play with your fingers, on iPhone, iPad and Mac, and inside GarageBand.

The pitch

RedSynth Pad is a synthesizer that takes its sound from a photo. Choose a picture and the app takes it apart in front of you: its patterns become the tones that ring when you strike it, its columns become the tone that sings while you hold a note, its colour becomes a hint of a vowel, and its five main colours light the window. Then you play it with your fingers, on an iPhone's or iPad's screen or a Mac's trackpad, over lanes of a scale, chords, drums, the photo itself, or a one-finger effect pad. Or from a MIDI keyboard with no trackpad at all, or keys and the trackpad's effect pad at the same time. A drum kit, a sampler and a loop deck make loops and beats.

It doesn't find a hidden sound in a photo: it's a mapping designed for the app, where each part of the picture sets one part of an instrument. The struck tones are modal synthesis, and a space is a model block whose vibrations are solved.

Add a second photo and the two become a 3D space: a block you strike, with a loop of light you move through it by hand. Then Sculpt opens the sound itself: paint on a photo, or paint materials into the block, and hear every stroke as you make it.

It is one purchase for iPhone, iPad and Mac with no subscription, and the same instrument ships as an AUv3 Audio Unit for Audio Unit hosts on both, checked so far in GarageBand on the Mac. It collects no data and has no network access. It was made by one person in Kentucky, on a sound engine written from scratch in Rust.

How a photo becomes a sound

  • Patterns become tones. The app finds the repeating patterns in the picture. Each one becomes a tone that rings when the object is struck, up to forty.
  • Columns become the voice. Read left to right, the picture's columns are the tone that sings while a note is held.
  • Colour becomes a hint of a vowel. One resonance set by the photo's average colour: pink sings a bright "ee", green a round "oo".
  • Every pixel signs it. A signature worked out from every pixel sets the fine details. Change a pixel and they change; the same photo always makes the same instrument.
  • The palette becomes light. Five colours from the photo are laid round the circle of fifths, so a sweet chord sits on one side of the wheel and a tense one spans it. Scriabin's colour keyboard used the same circle-of-fifths idea.

The story

Seth Egger is a musician and painter who writes software. RedSynth began as a synthesizer he built for himself and played from a trackpad. A small experiment that turned a photo into knob settings sounded good, so the photo became the instrument.

Seth builds his apps with Claude Code as his coding partner. The product calls are his.
